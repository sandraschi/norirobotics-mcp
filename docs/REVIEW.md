# Nori A3 — Field Review

This is an evaluative pass, not a spec sheet — for the full numbers see [`nori_info`](../src/norirobotics_mcp/knowledge.py)'s `NORI_HERO`, and for the philosophical case behind the form factor see [HOME_ROBOT_PARADIGM.md](HOME_ROBOT_PARADIGM.md). This doc exists to say plainly what's good, what's missing, and where the marketing outruns the spec sheet — written from a desk, pre-hardware, against the sourced material this repo already has (see [WRAPPEE.md](WRAPPEE.md) for citations). Treat the gaps section as "absent from every sourced spec and the launch thread," not "confirmed absent" — worth a direct check with Nori Robotics before either buying or ruling it out on these grounds.

## Verdict, upfront

The core bet — wheeled base, telescoping column, two arms, cloud-mediated compute — is the right one for a standard apartment, and at $1,688 it's priced like a consumer appliance rather than a research instrument, which is the whole point. The gaps below are real and mostly boring (charging, payload, actuator class), not fatal to the concept. This reads as a genuinely good v1, not a finished product.

## The case for: why this form factor is right

**Legs are a solution to a problem apartments don't have.** A standard flat is a flat-floored, cluttered, human-scaled box. The only thing legs buy you over wheels is stairs and rough terrain — and a home interior has neither, except the occasional threshold a ramp or a decent caster handles for free. Everything else legs bring — dynamic balance, a dozen extra actuators, a robot that can fall over, a bipedal gait stack that costs more than the rest of the robot combined — is pure overhead in a living room. Legged home robots are slow, expensive, and over-engineered for a problem that mostly doesn't exist indoors. Wheels are not the cheap compromise here; they're the *correct* answer, and legs are the one that needs justifying.

**The telescoping column is the actual unlock, not a workaround.** A fixed-height wheeled base is a novelty — it can reach one shelf. Nori A3's three-stage column (69–145 cm, 76 cm of travel) turns "wheeled base" into "wheeled base that can reach the floor and the counter and most of what's between them," which is the difference between a gimmick and an appliance. It's a mechanically boring, well-understood mechanism (a stage lift, a pallet jack mast) doing the job a much more expensive leg assembly would otherwise have to do. This single design choice is what multiplies the plausible task list from "fetch things at counter height" to "load the dishwasher, reach the low shelf, put something on the counter" — pick-and-place across the whole vertical range a kitchen or living room actually uses, without a single extra DOF in the base or arms.

**The price is the product.** $1,688 lands in iPhone territory, not car-or-research-grant territory. Comparable bimanual manipulation platforms have historically lived at $20k–$100k+ — accessible to a lab, not a household. A robot that costs about the same as a good phone is the difference between "something a hobbyist or an early adopter can actually buy" and "something a university buys one of." That price point is also what gets enough units into enough real homes to generate the usage data this entire category is starved for — a research platform in a lab generates none of that.

**The software stack is genuinely open, not open-in-name.** Apache-2.0 SDK, real GitHub presence ([`nori-sdk-py`](https://github.com/Nori-Robotics/nori-sdk-py)), LeRobot-compatible dataset format so existing Hugging Face training pipelines apply without conversion, and lineage from one of the more active open robotics communities around (XLeRobot/SO-ARM100, 4.8k+ stars). For a company this size, that's the only economically sane move — a $2–5k robot cannot fund an in-house software team the size a $100k research platform can, so opening the stack borrows a much bigger community's effort instead of trying to out-build it alone. It also means this MCP server, and anyone else's tooling, can exist at all; a closed SDK would have made this whole repo impossible.

## The gaps

**No charging station, anywhere in the sourced material.** Not in the spec sheet, not in the launch thread, not in the SDK docs this repo has read. A 432 Wh battery good for 6–8 hours is fine — but a home appliance that needs a human to notice it's low and manually plug it in every evening is a chore robot that creates a new chore. Every successful home robot category (vacuums, mowers) won this exact problem with autonomous docking; its total absence from the launch material for an otherwise appliance-positioned product is conspicuous, not just an oversight worth a footnote.

**Payload is genuinely light, not just "not heavy-duty."** 1.5 kg per arm is real for a cup, a book, a folded shirt — and real about **not** being enough for a laundry basket, a case of water, a full grocery bag, or a stack of plates. The 76 cm of lift travel solves *reach*, not *load*. Anyone reading "telescoping lift" as "can move heavy things vertically" is reading more into the mechanism than the spec supports — it repositions a 1.5 kg-capable arm, it doesn't turn it into a hand truck.

**Cheap-and-cheerful actuators, and the Hacker News crowd already said so.** Feetech STS-series bus servos are RC-hobbyist-grade, position-controlled, not the quasi-direct-drive/integrated-BLDC actuators (CubeMars, MyActuator — both named on the launch thread) that give real torque/current sensing and higher control bandwidth. This is a legitimate precision and durability ceiling, not snobbery — the whole reason Nori's own actuator-protection layer exists in software (thermal/stall protection via `Present_Current` polling, not a real torque sensor) is to compensate for exactly this. It's a reasonable BOM trade at this price, but it is a trade, and the shoulder/lift axes (STS3095's 95 kg·cm ceiling) are the most likely place it shows up first as a real limitation, not a theoretical one.

**No lean, no torso pitch — the column only goes up and down.** The three-stage lift is documented as purely vertical travel; nothing in the spec sheet or research pass mentions a waist joint, a forward-pitching torso, or any way for the robot to lean its reach envelope forward the way a human bends at the hips to get into the back of a deep cabinet. 55 cm of arm reach from a column that can move up and down but never forward-and-down means the effective working envelope is a vertical slab in front of the base, not a hemisphere the way a human's is. Reaching to the back of a larder, behind something on a counter, or into a front-loading appliance set back from its own door frame is exactly the class of task this geometry doesn't obviously cover — and it's the kind of gap that only shows up once you're trying to do the task, not when you're reading the spec sheet.

**The control channel is cloud-mediated, not local.** WebRTC + Supabase Realtime signaling means there's no documented local-only operation mode — a real dependency on an internet connection and a third party's infrastructure for a robot operating inside your home, and a real privacy/telemetry question the launch thread itself raised and never fully answered.

**The demo-vs-reality gap the launch thread already flagged.** Some HN commenters read the clothes-folding demo as closer to "throwing into a pile" than an actual controlled fold. Worth remembering that launch demos are marketing, and this one hasn't had independent scrutiny yet — a healthy prior for any pre-shipment hardware, not specific skepticism about Nori in particular.

## Bottom line

None of the gaps above argue against the core concept — they argue that this is a first-generation product at a genuinely disruptive price, and it shows in exactly the places a $1,688 BOM would predict: charging, payload, actuator class, and torso articulation. The wheels-not-legs, telescoping-column, open-SDK bet is the right one; whether Nori A3 specifically is the robot that proves it out, or just the first honest attempt at the right shape, is the actual open question — and it's one only real hardware in real homes will answer, not another spec sheet.
